The Zarankiewicz number z(13, 19; 3, 3) equals 120

Research note
Oleg Fedorenko · Aperio Bar Association
25 October 2026 · version 1 · data, code and audit logs: github.com/Oleg-Fedorenko/zarankiewicz-13x19 · doi:10.5281/zenodo.23637633
Abstract. The Zarankiewicz number z(m, n; 3, 3) is the largest number of ones in an m × n 0/1 matrix with no 3 × 3 submatrix of ones. We prove z(13, 19; 3, 3) = 120. Matrices with 120 ones were known; the best published upper bound was 122. The proof that no such matrix has 121 ones is computer-assisted and certified throughout. A counting argument leaves 30 column-degree profiles. Twenty-six are refuted by SAT solving with DRAT proofs. The four most uniform profiles are split into 2 478 anchor cubes — by how the other columns meet a column of maximum degree — and the hardest cubes into 58 186 sub-cubes by fixing further columns in canonical order. Each sub-cube is refuted either by an exact rational Farkas certificate for a linear relaxation (32 602 of them) or by SAT with a DRAT or LRAT proof. An audit regenerated every formula, compared it byte for byte with the certified one, and re-verified every Farkas certificate in exact arithmetic. On the way we found that the LRAT checker lrat-check accepts an unjustified final step; we used a strict variant. All data, code and logs are public.

1Result

The values of z(m, n; 3, 3) for m, n ≤ 18 were tabulated by Collins, Riasanovsky, Wallace and Radziszowski [9], building on the classical bound of Kővári, Sós and Turán [19]; recent work raised many lower bounds and settled several values beyond that range [1–8, 10]. The cell (13, 19) was open.

Theorem 1. z(13, 19; 3, 3) = 120.

Lower bound. A 13 × 19 matrix with 120 ones and no 3 × 3 block of ones is in the repository (witness/z-13-19-120.txt; row degrees 12, 912, column degrees 8, 74, 614); it was found by the search laboratory of AperioBot (§9), in which a language model writes the search programs, independently of Norris’s matrix [7]. Upper bound. Sections 2–6 show that no admissible 13 × 19 matrix has 121 ones; this suffices, since deleting ones keeps a matrix admissible. Table 1 lists what was known.

On [11]. This SSRN preprint lists z(13, 19; 3) = 120 among “44 new exact values”, with no proof or data. Of its twelve exact values in rows 12–14, eight are contradicted by published matrices with more ones — for instance it gives z(13, 20) = 124, while a matrix with 125 ones has been public since 2025 [8] — and its z(14, 18) = 115 contradicts its own z(13, 18) ≥ 116 (a row with two ones can always be added). Its Zenodo deposit was deleted as retracted. The value 120 there is therefore an assertion, not a result; we regard the proof below as the first.

2Reduction to 30 profiles

Write ri and cj for row and column degrees, C(a, b) for binomial coefficients, and call a matrix admissible if it has no 3 × 3 block of ones, i.e. any three rows share at most two columns and any three columns share at most two rows. Suppose A is admissible, 13 × 19, with 121 ones.

Lemma 1 (deletion). Deleting a rows and b columns of A leaves an admissible (13−a) × (19−b) matrix, so the deleted lines carry at least 121 − z(13−a, 19−b) ones (a one at a crossing counted once).

We use eight upper bounds: z(13,18) ≤ 116, z(13,17) ≤ 110, z(13,16) ≤ 107, z(12,19) ≤ 114, z(11,19) ≤ 106, z(10,19) ≤ 98, z(12,18) ≤ 108 and z(11,17) ≤ 96. All follow from Table 4 of Collins, Riasanovsky, Wallace and Radziszowski [9] by the single-line case of Lemma 1 (a lightest line of an m × n matrix with Z ones has at most ⌊Z/n⌋ ones, so Z − ⌊Z/n⌋ ≤ z(m, n−1)), except z(12, 18) ≤ 108, which we proved separately: under cuts from [9] alone, a 12 × 18 matrix with 109 ones has the single column profile (7, 617), refuted by Glucose 4.2.1 [16] with a 2.7 GB DRAT proof verified by drat-trim [14] (it is also claimed in [3, 4]). With it, the upper bound rests on [9] and on our certificates only.

Lemma 1 with single lines gives: every k columns of A hold at least 5, 11, 14 ones and every k rows at least 7, 15, 23 ones (k = 1, 2, 3). Every triple of rows lies in at most two columns, so Σj C(cj, 3) ≤ 2 C(13, 3) = 572. Exactly 30 multisets of column degrees with sum 121 satisfy these conditions (two independent enumerations agree). Each defines a case. Its formula has a variable per cell, a clause per choice of three rows and three columns forbidding nine ones, cardinality constraints fixing the column degrees and imposing the row cuts (one totalizer per row), and symmetry breaking: rows in lexicographic order, columns grouped by degree and lexicographically ordered within a degree class. This is sound because sorting rows, or swapping two adjacent equal-degree columns that violate the order, increases the matrix read row by row as a binary word, so a maximal element of every orbit satisfies all of it.

Twenty-six cases were refuted whole by Glucose 4.2.1 (-no-pre -certified), each DRAT proof “s VERIFIED” by drat-trim (hardest: 8 110 s of solving, 5 099 s of checking). The four most uniform profiles,

A = (8, 75, 613),  B = (8, 76, 611, 5),  C = (78, 610, 5),  D = (77, 612),

did not finish in 12.5 hours. For them we strengthen the formula and split the case.

3Mixed deletion cuts and anchor cubes

Lemma 2 (mixed cuts). For every row i and column j, ri + cj − xij ≥ 121 − z(12, 18) ≥ 13, and for rows i, i′ and columns j, j′, the four lines minus the ones at their four crossings carry at least 121 − z(11, 17) ≥ 25 ones.

This is Lemma 1 with (a, b) = (1, 1) and (2, 2). For instance, in profile C every row has at least 8 ones (C has a column of degree 5), and in every profile a row with 7 ones has no one in a column of degree 6. Adding these clauses made the solver 3–7 times faster on the cases below.

Anchor cubes. In the canonical form the first column has the maximum degree c and, rows being lexicographic with that column most significant, its ones fill rows 1, …, c =: B. For every other column j let ij = |colj ∩ B| and oj = cj − ij.

Lemma 3. Every admissible matrix of the case satisfies
(I)Σj C(ij, 3) ≤ C(c, 3),(III)Σj C(ij, 2) oj ≤ 2 C(c, 2) (13−c),
(II)Σj C(oj, 3) ≤ 2 C(13−c, 3),(IV)Σj ij C(oj, 2) ≤ 2c C(13−c, 2),
and Σij lies in a window fixed by the row cuts (the rows of B carry at least c(cut1+1) − 1 ones when cut2 = 2cut1 + 1, and likewise the other rows).

Each sum counts row triples of a given kind (inside B, outside, two inside and one out, one in and two out) by the columns containing them; each triple lies in at most two columns, and a triple inside B already lies in the first column. A pattern is, for each degree class of the other columns, the multiset of their ij; a cube is the case formula plus “the first column is B” plus the pattern’s counts (for each class and threshold t, how many columns have ij ≥ t). Every canonical matrix lies in the cube of its own pattern, and all patterns meeting Lemma 3 are listed (two independent enumerations agree): A 203, B 512, C 1 254, D 509 cubes. Kissat 4.0.4 (--unsat, binary DRAT, drat-trim) refuted all cubes of A and B and 1 065 of C and D whole; for the other 698 the proof outgrew what drat-trim can hold in 16 GB.

4Sub-cubes

Lemma 4 (canonical column fixing). In the canonical form, once columns 0, …, k−1 are fixed, the rows fall into groups with equal entries in those columns, each group consecutive and lexicographically ordered in the remaining columns. Hence column k has its ones at the start of every group and is determined by the number of its ones in each group.

So the choices for columns 1, …, d form a finite tree of count vectors; we prune it only with necessary conditions — the column’s degree and the pattern’s remaining counts, at most two common rows with any two fixed columns, and lexicographic order among equal-degree columns. The leaves at depth d (sub-cubes) cover the cube. C’s 467 split cubes gave 28 845 sub-cubes (43, 15 249, 13 553 at depths 1, 2, 3); D’s 231 gave 29 341 (20, 7 212, 21 756, and 353 at depth 4 under one sub-cube that took more than an hour at depth 3). A sub-cube is the cube’s formula with the fixed columns as unit clauses.

5A linear relaxation with exact Farkas certificates

We extend Afrasyab’s block polytope [3, §5], which fixes one column, to all fixed columns of a sub-cube. Group the rows by their entries in the fixed columns; let ng be the size of group g. The type τ of a remaining column records its number of ones in each group; let yd,τ ≥ 0 be the number of remaining columns of degree d and type τ. Every matrix of the sub-cube gives an integral solution of

  • (E) for each degree class and each value v: the columns of that class with v ones in B number exactly what the pattern says, less the fixed ones;
  • (T) for each multiset t of groups (an orbit of row triples): Σ yd,τ Πg C(τg, tg) ≤ (2 − κt) Πg C(ng, tg), where κt is the number of fixed columns containing such triples;
  • (G) for each group: its rows carry at least f·ng ones, where f = max(121 − z(12, 19), maxj(121 − z(12, 18) − cj)) is the row floor of Lemmas 1–2 (7 for D, 8 for C).

If this system Ay ≤ b, Ey = e, y ≥ 0 has no real solution, Farkas’ lemma gives α ≥ 0 and β with ATα + ETβ ≥ 0 and bTα + eTβ < 0, and the sub-cube is empty. We find a sparse such vector with HiGHS [17] (a vertex of an ℓ1-minimizing LP), make it rational, and verify it in exact integer arithmetic; the vector is the certificate. 32 602 sub-cubes (C 17 359, D 15 243) were closed this way without a solver. The fixed columns are essential: with the first column alone the relaxation is invariant under the row permutations fixing it, and every anchor pattern defines a feasible point (we checked all 2 478 in exact arithmetic), so no certificate of this kind exists at cube level.

6SAT with proofs, and a gap in an LRAT checker

The other 25 584 sub-cubes were refuted by Kissat 4.0.4 with drat-trim, or, from 23 October, by CaDiCaL 3.0.1 [13] writing an LRAT proof [15] into a pipe read by a checker while the proof is written (8 504 sub-cubes): no proof on disk, and 1.4–1.8 times faster than Kissat plus drat-trim on medium parts. A part not refuted within 25 minutes at depth 2 (60 at depth 3) was split one column deeper.

The checker. The LRAT checker lrat-check of the drat-trim repository (commit 2e3b2dc0ecf9) reports “c VERIFIED” for the satisfiable formula {x1, x1 ∨ x2} with the one-line proof “3 0 0”, an empty clause with no hints. A clause whose hints yield no conflict is accepted when no clause contains the negation of its first literal (a pure-literal test); for the empty clause that literal does not exist and the test succeeds. Clause identifiers are also read into 32-bit integers (an identifier of 5·109 wraps around and verifies). We found this before using the checker for any certificate, and used lrat-check-strict (in the repository): identical except that every added clause, the empty one included, must follow by unit propagation through its hints, and out-of-range numbers are rejected. CaDiCaL’s proofs of our known-UNSAT parts verify under it, so they contain no RAT steps. Every LRAT result records the SHA-256 of both binaries, and the audit accepts LRAT results only after the checker refuses the bogus proof above.

7Audit

An audit, separate from the runs, rechecked the whole proof from the generator (25 October 2026, log in the repository): (i) the 30 profiles are exactly those the counting admits; (ii) for each whole profile, the formula regenerated today equals the certified one byte for byte, and drat-trim re-verified every kept proof; (iii) for each anchor profile, the patterns were re-enumerated and equal the stored list; every cube’s choices at its deepest depth were re-enumerated and each lies in or below a certified sub-cube; every Farkas certificate was re-verified exactly; every solver part’s formula was regenerated and compared. Seventy-two proofs drawn at random were re-verified from scratch (DRAT re-checks and LRAT re-solves). No problem was found.

8Data and code

The repository github.com/Oleg-Fedorenko/zarankiewicz-13x19, archived as doi:10.5281/zenodo.23637633, contains the generator and checkers (Python, with PySAT [18]); the build recipe and hashes of the solvers and checkers, with lrat-check-strict.c; for every cube and sub-cube, its record (route, solver, checker, verdict, times) and the SHA-256 of its certified formula; all Farkas certificates; the audit logs; the witness; and a standalone verifier that repeats (i)–(iii) of §7 from the repository alone and re-solves random parts. The DRAT proofs (577 GB compressed) are kept by the author and available on request; LRAT proofs were checked while written and are not stored; any part can be regenerated and re-proved in minutes.

In addition to the main result, the repository includes explicit witness matrices establishing certified lower bounds for 18 adjacent cells (for instance z(14, 23) ≥ 146, z(15, 22) ≥ 150 and z(15, 23) ≥ 156; the list is in witness/README.md). They were found independently and reproduce, cell by cell, the best bounds that [7, 8] obtained by heuristic search, and they exceed the bounds of [1, 2].

Consequences. With Theorem 1, Lemma 1 gives z(14, 19; 3, 3) ≤ 129 (128 is attained [7]); the same method is being applied to 14 × 19.
